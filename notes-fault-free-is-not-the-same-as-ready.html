Fault-free is not the same as ready, right?

19 November 2026 · Ir. Ts. Dr. Bibi Sabrena Sakandar Khan

Malaysia has several rail and transit openings coming: the RTS Link, Kuching's ART, and later Johor's e-ART. Each one will face the same pressure to open the moment the system runs clean and smooth.

One recent airport APM in Malaysia shows what that pressure can cost. It reopened in mid-2025 after fault-free running and only a few days of ORAT (Operational Readiness and Airport Transfer). Within four months it had logged about 19 reported disruptions, most of them minor. It then went into nightly closures for a recovery plan that included trial operations and endurance tests. Full 24-hour service returned 11 months after opening. The independent assessors later described the disruptions as common in the early operational phase of a complex rail system, and the system as functioning as intended.

I agree with them. The system and the people running it needed time to mature together. Integration, and the time it takes for people and machine to settle in with each other, has to be planned for. That is what protects the asset's life cycle for the next 20 to 25 years.

That time was taken after opening, in public, when it could have been taken before.

There was pressure, nerves running high, and assumptions and accusations that someone, somewhere, had failed. A project that should take 3 to 5 years gets squeezed into less. The reason? Pressure.

I see them. Everywhere. 👀

Five observations for any critical asset project

Having sat in a project director's seat, here are five observations. They are general, and I offer them mildly.

1. Treat the business case as urgent. Once an asset has reached the end of its life, questions like "can we stretch it with a small upgrade?" or "something else matters more this year" only move the risk to a later and more expensive date.

2. Projects complement each other. I have heard "your project is simple, ours is complicated" more than once. Ask what the other team needs, and be curious before you advocate anything.

3. Be realistic. "Compress it into three years", "we cannot afford a shutdown" and "we will think about safety during execution" are all decisions about time, safety and cost. Make them openly at the start, with the numbers on the table.

4. Hold the line under pressure. Every project has issues, and solving them takes time and effort. Opening first and concluding later is the point where many project managers and directors give in, especially on safety and integrity.

5. Use independent assessors properly. Appoint a team with the right specialists, a clear objective and enough time to look at the whole system.

Guts, Grit and Gusto

That is what Guts, Grit and Gusto means to me. Guts to say "not yet" to people who outrank you. Grit to do the slow integration work nobody sees. Gusto to make the team own the new system before the first passenger boards.

I have plenty of lessons learnt. If you are preparing an asset for service, I am happy to compare notes.
