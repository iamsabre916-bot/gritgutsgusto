Two papers, zero bonus: an industry practitioner at WiSET 2026

27 November 2026 · Ir. Ts. Dr. Bibi Sabrena Sakandar Khan

At the start of 2026 I gave myself a KPI: one conference paper. In twenty years of airport and infrastructure work I had never published one, and I was tired of saying "one day".

The result: two papers accepted at WiSET 2026, the 4th International Conference for Women in Science, Engineering & Technology, held at Casuarina @ Meru, Perak from 23 to 25 November. Both were presented by my co-authors. I was not in the room.

So I hit 200% of target. In every organisation I have worked for, that would start a bonus conversation. As a sole proprietor, my scorecard is set by me, assessed by me and approved by me, and the bonus came to exactly RM0. For 2027 I am upgrading to a proper scorecard on myself, with targets, weightage and a mid-year review. Still no bonus. The HR department of one has spoken.

The two papers

Digitalization Begins with Simplicity: Lessons from Implementing an Electronic Document Management System in Airport Project Delivery (Paper ID 3), co-authored with Ir. Ts. Nurul Hafeeza Mohamed, PMP, who presented it. Nurul and I set up the first use of an electronic document management system in our organisation, on a live airport project. Moving from paper files and email attachments to one controlled system sounds boring. It is boring. It is also the reason a project can answer "which drawing is the latest?" in seconds instead of days. The lesson is in the title: digitalisation does not start with a big platform. It starts with making one simple thing work properly, and letting people see the difference.

Starting Small, Scaling Impact: How a Women-Led Team Closed an Engineering Visibility Gap (Paper ID 7), co-authored with Ir. Wan Adawiah Wan Asman Shukor, PMP, who presented it. Engineering had no real-time view of asset performance. Data sat in different places, the main enterprise system was built mostly for financial planning, and regulatory quality-of-service requirements kept rising. A small team of women did not wait for a big system. They built dashboards with free tools, showed what the data could do, and the effort kept growing from there. The reviewers gave the abstract three "strong accept" and two "accept". Not bad for a team that started with free software and a lot of stubbornness.

Why "women-led" matters here

Neither paper is about women for the sake of it. Both are about critical assets that run 24 hours a day. But in both cases the change was started by women who were close to the problem, did not have the title or the budget to order a big system, and fixed it anyway. Small, practical, documented, then scaled.

That pattern is common in engineering teams, and it is rarely written up. The person who designs the workaround moves on to the next crisis. The workaround becomes "how we do things here". Years later nobody remembers who started it, or why it worked.

The bill nobody puts in a KPI

I never thought publishing a paper would cost this much. Registration, travel and a hotel for a three-day conference add up. When I had a steady salary I would not have looked twice. As a woman entrepreneur just starting a business, every ringgit now has a job, and this one did not make the cut.

So I checked how academics manage it every year. Mostly, they do not pay from their own pocket. University conference funds and research grants, such as the Fundamental Research Grant Scheme (FRGS), commonly cover registration and travel for staff who present. That makes sense, because publishing is part of their scorecard.

For industry practitioners, it is not. I have never seen a corporate KPI that says "publish what you learned". So engineers, project managers and operators with twenty years of hard-won lessons retire with all of it in their heads, their laptops and a few old WhatsApp groups. Such a waste.

How to get practitioners writing

If I were giving the keynote, this is what I would ask for:

  • Put "document and share one lesson a year" into practitioners' scorecards, with the same weight as closing an audit finding.
  • Give industry practitioners a lower fee to present, or let companies sponsor a paper the way they sponsor a booth.
  • Pair a practitioner with an academic. One brings the real case, the other brings the method, and both get a paper.
  • Start small. A short extended abstract of up to three pages is a good first target. Both of ours started that way.

The ripple effect is real. A documented lesson gets cited, then copied, then becomes someone else's starting point instead of their mistake. The women in these two papers made small changes to critical assets. Written down, those changes can help far more people than the teams who made them.

Thank you, and a question

Thank you to Nurul and Adawiah for carrying both papers to the stage, and to the WiSET organisers for letting co-authors present on my behalf.

Now my question to you: do you know of good conferences or seminars where industry practitioners can present for free, or for a small fee? I have more papers in me, and a 2027 scorecard to hit.

Know a conference that welcomes practitioners?

Tell me about it, or talk to me about training that turns your team's lessons into something written down and shared.
